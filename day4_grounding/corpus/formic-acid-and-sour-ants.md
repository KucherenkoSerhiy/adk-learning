# Why Some Ants Taste Sour

The sourness in many ant species comes from formic acid, a compound whose
name literally derives from the Latin word for ant, *formica*. Formic acid is
produced and stored in a gland connected to the ant's venom apparatus, and in
most ant species that gland sits in the gaster — the bulbous rear segment of
an ant's body. Note that "gaster" is the anatomically correct term, not
"abdomen": ants, like other Hymenoptera (bees, wasps), have a narrow waist
called a petiole separating the mesosoma (thorax-equivalent) from the gaster,
so the gaster is not a true abdomen in the strict entomological sense.

Species known for a genuinely citrus-sour taste, driven primarily by formic
acid, include common black ants (*Lasius niger* and relatives), harvester
ants, and wood ants (genus *Formica* — the same genus the acid is named
after). The sourness is sharp and bright, frequently compared directly to
lemon juice or lemonade, sometimes with a bergamot-like brightness in
harvester ants specifically linked to terpenes in their diet layered on top
of the formic acid itself.

This is not just folk trivia — the citrus-like sharpness of formic acid has
real culinary use. High-end chefs have used live ants (most famously at Noma
in Copenhagen) as a citrus substitute or garnish, since the tartness
transfers well into liquids and creates a tang that's difficult to replicate
with conventional ingredients. Eating a live wood ant and biting down
produces an immediate, sharp citrus hit for exactly this reason.
