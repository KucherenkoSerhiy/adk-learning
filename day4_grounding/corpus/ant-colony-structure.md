# Ant Colony Structure

An ant colony is organized into castes, and every individual within a caste
is female except for one group. The queen (or queens, in polygynous species)
is the primary egg-layer and typically founds the colony; in most species
she mates only once, early in life, storing sperm for the rest of her
lifespan, which can run to a decade or more in some species — long for an
insect. Workers are sterile females and make up the vast majority of the
colony: they forage, build and maintain the nest, tend to the brood, and
defend the colony. Males, often called drones, exist almost solely to mate
with new queens during nuptial flights and typically die shortly afterward;
they contribute essentially no labor to the colony.

Many species show further division within the worker caste through
polymorphism — physically distinct sub-castes suited to different jobs.
Leafcutter ants (genus *Atta*, the same genus as hormigas culonas) are a
well-known example: minor workers tend fungus gardens and care for brood,
while major workers ("soldiers") have disproportionately large heads and
mandibles suited to defense and cutting tougher vegetation. Army ant
colonies similarly rely on a soldier sub-caste for colony defense during
their nomadic phases.

Colony size varies enormously by species — from a few dozen individuals in
some primitive ant lineages to colonies of many millions in species like
driver ants and some invasive supercolonies, where a single colony can span
enormous areas through interconnected nests. This scale, combined with the
degree of coordinated behavior across the colony, is why ant colonies are
frequently described as superorganisms: the colony as a whole behaves in
some ways more like a single organism than a simple aggregation of
individuals.
